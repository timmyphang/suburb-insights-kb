# Woolloongabba (QLD)

Slug: `woolloongabba-qld`

Back in the day — and by that we mean all the back-catalogue clippings that love reminding us we’re “home of The Gabba” — Woolloongabba felt like a weekday romcom with a surprise finale. We’d shuffle between the antique bones of the Moreton Rubber Works (where 1889 Enoteca set up its pasta shrine) and Stanley Street, which used to be the place for C’est Bon breakfasts and Fiasco’s steaks when we felt suitably dramatic.

Mornings, we remember, started with a lap of the South City/SQ precinct at 148–160 Logan Road, where markets and Spring Fling-type activations used to pass for wholesome civic theatre. By lunch we’d be sampled lists — Broadsheet and Urban List loved reminding us about Sasso Italiano, Detour and Electric Avenue’s bar scene — and by night we’d whisper about hidden bars: Mrs J’s Rabbits tucked inside Electric Avenue, D. Vino waiting on 77 Jurgens Street, and Uncle Don cropping up in reviews like a reliable plot device.

The long-running plot twist? The Gabba itself. Papers from Concrete Playground and Urban List turned stadium gossip into destiny: demolition, a 17,000-seat arena plan and a 2032-shaped makeover that would see teams relocated and half our weekend rituals disrupted. Add Urban List’s caution about limited event-day parking and the Pedestrian TV reminders about public-transport strain during mega-gigs, and suddenly our quiet corner looked like a construction lottery.

Who would hate living here? People who obsess over quiet weekends, pristine parking and zero crane ballet. If the thought of being elbow-to-elbow with matchday pilgrims or swapping your Saturday sleep-in for a road closed sign fills you with rage, Woolloongabba is sadly not the vibe.

Who would love it? We’d still recommend it in the sort of wistful way you recommend an old neighbourhood: for food obsessives who pored over the “best restaurants” lists, for secret-bar hunters who delight in addresses like 23 Logan Road, for art lovers who used to drop by the Woolloongabba Art Gallery and for anyone who thinks urban reinvention is its own thrilling mess. We’re messy, loud and deliciously ready for whatever demolition brings next — and frankly, we’ve always liked it that way.
