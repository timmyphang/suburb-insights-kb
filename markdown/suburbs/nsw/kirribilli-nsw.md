# Kirribilli (NSW)

Slug: `kirribilli-nsw`

We’ll start the day like proper locals — tiptoeing under the McDougall Street jacarandas when they turn that ridiculous violet in spring, trying to dodge someone else’s influencer shoot. For years the guidebooks have been full of this stuff, so we’re mostly reminiscing rather than pretending we’ve got a fresh scoop.

Back in the day Kirribilli’s village strip was the sort of place you could name-check and sound cultured: Celsius at Kirribilli Wharf had that Hamptons-ish makeover and those harbour views (Billy’s eggs, beef‑brisket eggs benedict with yuzu hollandaise — yes, really), Anvil Coffee Co bragged about a “Fat Poppy” blend, and Cool Mac on Burton Street used to pour Campos while Jun Okamatsu tinkered with Japanese-inspired dishes. The Kirribilli Hotel’s art‑deco face and beloved back terrace were always a comforting sight on Broughton Street, and Small Bar & Kitchen used to keep the North Shore’s cocktail fans entertained at 1–3 Broughton.

We used to gossip about the markets in the Burton Street Tunnel — the Kirribilli Art, Design & Fashion Market that ran on the second Sunday each month, organised by the Kirribilli Neighbourhood Centre on Fitzroy Street. For a minute it felt like the whole suburb moonlighted as a craft fair: millinery, knitted things, laser‑etched phone cases — the lot. Pasture of Kirribilli on Fitzroy Street had a brief moment of sustainable-glam with things like blood orange kingfish ceviche and black mussels with kaffir lime, and Foys at the Sydney Flying Squadron (76 McDougall Street) reminded us that waterfront clubs could do both nostalgia and brunch — Foys upstairs, the Flying Bear cafe downstairs.

Our petty scandal? A single car space in Kirribilli once sold for $120,000. If you want proof we care passionately about small, local absurdities, that is it.

Who would hate living here: anyone who detests being politely photobombed by tourists beneath the jacarandas (as UrbanList helpfully warns, avoid 10am–2pm on weekends), or people allergic to craft markets and harbour-view humble-bragging.

Who would love it: people who want a harbourside village that smells faintly of jasmine and salt, where the coffee used to be very good, the pub’s got an art‑deco wink, and the second Sunday market felt like permission to buy another handmade thing. We’ll keep the coconuts and the nostalgia warm for you.
