# Leichhardt North (NSW)

Slug: `leichhardt-north-nsw`

Back in the day we’d stagger out onto Norton Street and pretend we weren’t still in our pyjamas because the espresso-scented nostalgia made us look presentable. Guides used to call us Sydney’s “Little Italy” — and you could still feel it in the faded awnings of the Italian Forum and the long shadow of Bar Italia, the red‑sauce institution Time Out said had been feeding us since 1952. Cafe Gioia was the reminder that our pizza memory runs deep, even if the neighbourhood keeps getting remixed.

Our weekend ritual used to be embarrassingly wholesome: drop the kids at the Leichhardt Park Aquatic Centre, duck into Berkelouw Books or the Library for a minute of culture, then argue on Norton Street about whether Spicetown Leichhardt’s 20‑plus vendors (Concrete Playground’s words, not ours) counted as dining or a very loud homework excuse. The Palace Cinema’s recent renovation used to be that thing everyone mentioned at morning drinks — “Remember when the seats were worse?” — as if anyone could.

We’ll admit: the press never gave us a proper scandal. The data’s more about menus and maps than melodrama, so there’s no town square feud to gossip about — just steady, stubborn character. Even the practical stuff made the cut: Broadsheet once noted Coles was delivering from a Leichhardt outlet on Uber Eats, which meant our grocery panic could be outsourced to an app and delayed rationality.

Who would hate living here? If you loathe local rituals — outdoor piazza vibes, mid-century trattoria comfort, and people who will absolutely tell you where the best wood‑fired pizza used to be — you’ll feel displaced. If you need never to see a bookshop, pool or a community park again, best look elsewhere.

Who would love it? Families, according to Inner West Mums, who like the playlist of pool lanes, cinemas and library story hours. Food obsessives who worship at Norton Street’s altar and the people who miss the old-school Italian joints. And anyone who appreciates a suburb that tells its story in menus, maps and modest renovations rather than headlines.

We’re not flashy. We’re fond, a little stubborn, and full of leftovers from restaurants that earned their reputations. Come for the appetite, stay for the history — or at least the argument about where it went.
