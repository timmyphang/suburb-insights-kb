# Clovelly (NSW)

Slug: `clovelly-nsw`

Back in the day — and by that we mean most of our clippings are from the archives — Clovelly always felt like someone’s carefully curated summer memory that never got put away.

We start our weekend at Clovelly Beach, that perfectly narrow bay with two concrete promenades and a shallow shore where kids learn to splash and everyone pretends they’re here for the snorkelling. Broadsheet remembered the resident groper “Bluey”; we remember pretending we’d spotted him just to sound local. The Geoff James Pool (the ocean baths) kept us honest when the sea was being dramatic.

Our village loop used to be gloriously predictable: a lap at the baths, a stroll along the Coastal Walk, then sample the bakery offerings that Broadsheet and Concrete Playground liked to brag about. We used to queue for Out of the Blue at 272 Clovelly Road and joke we’d died and gone to chips-and-burger heaven. Tuga Pastries and Village on Cloey were our pastry pets — flaky Portuguese tarts handed down through real family recipes. Madame & Yves — yes, Yves Scherrer’s place on Clovelly Road — used to make us feel like we were in a pastel Paris that forgot to charge for the view.

There was small‑town drama, too: Urban List once flagged a plan to scrap free beach parking, which felt like a betrayal of everything we’d ever parked for. Pedestrian TV reminded us how often beaches get closed — for social distancing, for shark scares, for reasons that made us collectively sigh and move the picnic to the park.

Who would hate living here? If you crave pulsing nightlife or anonymous high-rise living, Clovelly will feel like a retirement village that throws a mean fish-and-chip night. If you loathe prams, murals at the Surf Life Saving Club, or bouquets from The Botanic Lab, this is not your scene.

Who would love it? Families, snorkellers, people who still think barefoot bowls at the Clovelly Bowling & Recreation Club is peak social life, and anyone who treasures a small, seaside village where the bakery knows your order and the sea knows your name — even if most of our proof lives in old web pages.
