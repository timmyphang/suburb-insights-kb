# Yering (NSW)

Slug: `yering-nsw`

We live in Yering, and honestly, we still like to pretend our postcode is a period drama with good weather and an affinity for elm trees. Back in the day the papers told everyone anyway: Yering Station planted vines in 1838, so when we parade down the drive we’re literally walking through someone else’s century-long flex. As Broadsheet noted, you’re greeted by elm trees that have been growing since the 1800s, a pretty pond, and an arched building that used to seat 120 people for panoramic wine-viewing — very genteel, very postcard.

Our weekend routine used to be refreshingly predictable. We’d duck into the Yering Station Farmers Market — Melbourne Mums Group called it “arguably the most stunning location to host a farmers market anywhere in Victoria” — then graze our way over to Yarra Valley Dairy, which since 1994 has been doing artisan cow’s- and goat’s-milk cheeses. If we were feeling excessive, we'd remember Chateau Yering, the regal old hotel that Urban List once described as sitting on a 250‑acre estate with marble ensuites and 32 suites. It’s the kind of place that makes your phone camera feel underdressed.

There isn’t much petty local drama in the clippings — which, fine, makes us suspicious. Instead the narrative is: historic vineyards, boutique stays, and awards. Time Out even reported that Yarra Yering had been crowned Australia’s Top Winery in 2026 — three times now, apparently — so yes, we enjoy being quietly smug while someone else opens another bottle.

Who would hate living here? If you can’t stand alpacas grazing next to apple trees (yep, The George at Yering had those), if you loathe heritage mansions, or if you need late-night kebabs, this isn’t your angle. If you want anonymous high-rise energy, keep scrolling.

Who would love it here? Lovers of history, people who think a farmers market by an 1800s pond is a personality trait, cheese evangelists, and anyone who wants a weekend that smells of oak barrels and ambition. We’ll be here, pretending we always meant to move to the country. As Broadsheet, Time Out, and the rest gently remind you: it’s all been done beautifully for us.
