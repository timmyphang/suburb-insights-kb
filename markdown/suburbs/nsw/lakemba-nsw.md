# Lakemba (NSW)

Slug: `lakemba-nsw`

We know how Lakemba reads at 9pm: Haldon Street is a long, delicious argument the whole suburb’s having with itself. Back in the day the whole strip used to become Ramadan Nights — rows of stalls selling golden murtabak, knafeh that squeaked on your teeth and pani puri launched like tiny explosives of joy — and we’d turn up late and leave with our pockets full of burnt sugar and our phones full of food photos.

We grew up with those nights. Haldon Street used to be the thing that everyone in Sydney could point at and say, “You haven’t eaten properly until you’ve queued on that footpath.” Broadsheet, Time Out and Concrete Playground have all chronicled it — the market, the smell, the claim that more than a million people once shuffled past in a month. Concrete Playground even warned you about the parking and the shuttle buses; we warned you about the line for knafeh.

There’s been drama — not the kind we invent on the weekend, but proper civic drama. Reports noted Canterbury-Bankstown Council’s decision to drop ‘Ramadan’ from the market name and the opinion pieces complained of erasure; other coverage flagged concerns about disrespectful behaviour by some visitors. We won’t pretend council decisions and headlines don’t give you something to gossip about at the kebab shop — they do.

Who would hate living here? If you’re allergic to noise after dark, don’t move in. If you need suburban silence and zero spice, Lakemba will keep you awake with laughter, loud conversations and the scent of cumin at 2am. If your idea of culture is a quiet museum, you’ll be queuing for patience.

Who loves it here? Families who need local community — the Early Childhood Centre on 35 Croydon St and the Lakemba Indocilik playgroup used to gather on Thursdays — and anyone who worships at the Imam Ali bin Abi Taleb Mosque or simply wants the kind of food education that can only come from decades of late-night stalls. Island Dreams Cafe at 47 Haldon Street used to be one of the little addresses people pointed to on their walk home.

So yes, we’re loud, we’re messy, we argue with council, and we smell faintly of roasted almonds at midnight. Come for the knafeh, stay for the sense that you’ve walked into a place that refuses to be boring.
