# St Kilda East (NSW)

Slug: `st-kilda-east-nsw`

We’ll level with you: St Kilda East rarely announces itself with a brass band — it just quietly rearranges everyone’s Saturday. Back in the day Broadsheet called it “under‑the‑radar” for a reason: Alma Park for dog‑watching and sun‑soaking, little village feels along Chapel, Carlisle and Acland, and new digs like 97 Alma Road nudging the place towards slightly posher.

Our weekend moves are embarrassingly predictable. We’d stroll past the old Gattica site on 223 Carlisle Street (that long‑running café that closed in 2018), pop by the Hank Marvin Markets if they’re on, and sneak into Alma Park with a thermos and a vague plan. The St Kilda Playgroup lot meet at 67 Argyle Street (Betty Day Community Centre) — membership $30 and yes, booking is required every single time, because we like our playdates like our council meetings: organised.

For those who measured life in pints and patios, Concrete Playground and Urban List used to point at 184 Carlisle Street and say “behold”: The Local Taphouse — rooftop beer garden, heaters, 19 taps, two fireplaces and allegedly an indoor bocce court. Wayne Seberry even got quoted on the chicken parma, if you cared about that sort of thing. Zanini’s second outpost landed here in 2019 with Sicilian nostalgia and the kind of deep‑fried Nutella pizza dough that makes dentists weep.

There isn’t much local scandal in our files — which is either very wholesome or very boring, depending on your appetite for drama. What we do have are property listings that loudly flirt with “coveted” — Sitchu’s pages grunt about tram access, walking distance to Chapel Street, Balaclava and Windsor stations, and tight‑held 1930s Ardoch estate apartments. Translation: if you like being within a tram’s sigh of everything, you’ll feel smug here.

Who would hate it? You’ll not win friends if you crave anonymous suburbia or eternal silence — we’re compact, sociable and prone to a good bocce match or market morning. Who would love it? People who want parks, predictable pubs, and the small‑town convenience of everything being embarrassingly walkable. For years the guides have said it quietly works — and honestly, that suits us just fine.
