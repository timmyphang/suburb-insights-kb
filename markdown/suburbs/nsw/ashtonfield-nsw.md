# Ashtonfield (NSW)

Slug: `ashtonfield-nsw`

We start our mornings with a double-shot oat flat white from the Ashtonfield Coles café in one hand and Prado keys in the other, because presentation matters even if the backyard is a postage-stamp. We wear Lorna Jane to the school drop-off and a Target linen shirt to the Sunday BBQ down Willowmere Crescent. We own a Toyota Prado or Subaru Outback because two child seats in the back look official, and our Cavoodle’s Instagram is better curated than some local businesses.

Saturday is ritual: 6.30 Bootcamp at the community oval or a Pilates class at Jetts, a quick swing through Coles for organic kale, and a family “walk” at Parkview Avenue where the phones are for different reasons. Sunday is sacred: Bunnings for screws we’ll never use, fairy lights strung for a backyard movie night, and the cordless leaf blower polished like it’s a hobby rather than a war on leaves.

Our petty dramas are community theatre. The Coles carpark spat started as a comment thread and became a weeks-long Facebook war about the “best spot.” Someone printed “no overnight parking” signs and stapled them to every lamppost. Someone else accused a neighbour of stealing a garden gnome in an emoji-laden post that never mentioned the gnome’s political affiliation. The “reserved family parking” sign at the shopping centre produced threats of measuring tape and a midnight stakeout photo. Speed hump placement in our cul-de-sac on Meadowview Drive has reached constitutional-crisis levels: there were scones-and-tea subcommittee meetings, three competing flyers taped to letterboxes, and a flyer that may or may not have been laminated.

We post every school fundraiser on Facebook like it’s community currency. We own more reusable coffee cups than actual crockery. We insist our dog is “low-shed” while picking fluff out of the car like a forensic exam. We’ve got a soft-roader Ranger and a RAV4 that have never left Ashtonfield’s roundabouts, but “you never know” is a respectable excuse.

Who would hate living here? The city hipster with a single canvas tote and a disdain for tow bars would wilt. They’ll be baffled by grass-length thrasher arguments, bewildered by the altar of weekend backyard BBQs, and judged for cycling past without a Prado wave.

Who would love it? Mid-30s parents commuting to Newcastle or Maitland, lovers of neighbourly rows and charity bake stalls, people who want a safe cul-de-sac, good coffee, and the comforting hum of small-town theatrics — welcome home.
