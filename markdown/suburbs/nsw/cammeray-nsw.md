# Cammeray (NSW)

Slug: `cammeray-nsw`

Back in the day our press clippings were all pre-2024, so forgive the nostalgia — Cammeray’s glow-up lives in old reviews and reliable neighbourhood legends.

We stroll up Miller Street like we own the footpath — past 504 Miller Street, where Don’t Tell Uncle set up a posh sibling to a Surry Hills favourite, and past the windows where Folly’s Bar & Bistro pretended to be Amalfi Coast-adjacent. We used to queue for Maggio’s pastries (that long-running patisserie has been around since 1998 — Concrete Playground had notes on the cannoli and the much-loved Maggio’s Eggs). Broadsheet once told us The Alchemist Espresso was opened by two folks who literally re-mortgaged their house; you could spot it by an old Victoria Bitter sign and caffeine-molecule artworks. Nearby, Sushi Oe opened in 2020 and ran a tiny omakase — Broadsheet flagged an eight-seat experience that made every booking feel like a conspiracy.

There’s a neat little ecosystem in the clips: The Public and Wild Sage cropped up in round-ups, Laneway Café earned praise for its brunchy pull (Urban List) and Cammeray Craft got Time Out shoutouts for cheap beers that still felt precious. Hudson Meats was on the shopping list for people who get irrationally excited about cookbooks and condiments. Sitchu’s listings remind us Primrose Park keeps the greenery credible, and the Cammeray Children’s Centre has been the quiet heartbeat for local families for 35+ years — it only runs Tuesday to Friday, if you needed that intel. Mumspages even had Mini Chef Kids Cooking Classes on Lytton Street, because of course we outsource our kids’ tastebuds.

Petty drama? Our file is light. The only recurring subplot is “which tiny restaurant will close next” (Brewtown Cammeray and Ms. Miller were both listed as permanently closed — Broadsheet), so the local soap is mostly croissant-shaped.

Who’d hate living here: people who need a motorway of chain stores and big-venue anonymity; also anyone who can’t handle a neighbourhood where dinner bookings feel like theatre. Who’d love it: small-plate pilgrims, beer collectors, parents who appreciate a proper children’s centre, and anyone who secretly enjoys living in a place that reads like a food column from 2019 — charming, a touch smug, and still mostly walkable.
