# Glendenning (NSW)

Slug: `glendenning-nsw`

Back in the day we’d sling the kids in the car and head to the Glendenning Neighbourhood Centre on 48 Golding Drive like it was our civic ritual. We’re the people who know where the PlayConnect+ session used to run — Thursdays at 10:00 AM — and who’ve timed our Wednesdays around MyTime meet-ups (the listing showed a 9:30 AM weekly start, later noted in a term planner as Wednesdays 10:00–11:15 for TERM 3 2025). That’s our Saturday morning equivalent of queueing for brunch: corralling tiny humans and swapping the good local intel with other carers while the centre does the heavy lifting.

We’re not pretending Glendenning is a nightlife hub. Our brag is quieter: a neighbourhood that shows up. PlayConnect+ and MyTime aren’t flashy; they’re the reason people actually know each other here. They provide inclusive play and carer support for kids with disability or developmental concerns — and that practical, neighbourly stuff is what keeps our postcode moving.

If you’re the kind of person who moved here expecting estate-agent glamour, wine flights and sweeping vineyard views, you’d be better off following Lionel Glendenning’s name to the Hunter Valley — Concrete Playground points out he designed De Iuliis’ cellar door there — because that architectural-glass-and-cheese fantasy isn’t what we are. And yes, our surname’s had stranger cameos: Time Out’s review of Satan’s Slave (1976) remembers a Candace Glendenning in a much spookier context than our neighbourhood ever aspires to be.

Who would hate living here? People who need a late-night cocktail bar on every corner, people who think community support groups are boring, and anyone allergic to fluorescent community-hall lighting. Who would love it? Carers looking for MyTime support, parents who value inclusive PlayConnect+ sessions, and anyone who likes knowing their neighbours can actually help when life gets messy.

We’re not a postcard suburb. We’re the place with a neighbourhood centre on 48 Golding Drive where the real work of community happens — quietly, weekly, and very Australian about it.
