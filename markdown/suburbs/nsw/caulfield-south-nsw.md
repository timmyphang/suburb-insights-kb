# Caulfield South (NSW)

Slug: `caulfield-south-nsw`

We know this neighbourhood like the back of our scroll-sticky hands. Mornings here used to begin with the smell of butter and sugar on North Road — back in the day when Sebby’s Scrolls was our proud little local, baking from 3.30am and turning out what Broadsheet said were up to 2,500 cinnamon scrolls a week from its 367 North Road HQ. The takeaway window dramas, the sell-out signs, and the later leap to St Kilda? That’s Caulfield South hustle in miniature.

Weekends meant a loop: drop the kids at the Melbourne Montessori College parent‑toddler group at the Caulfield campus, detour to Princess Park for the dog off‑lead chaos, then stand in the Sebby’s queue that Sitchu and Urban List both swear “said it all.” If the scrolls were sold out, consolation came in the form of slow‑smoked meat memories from Big Boy BBQ at 764 Glenhuntly Road — the kind of ribs that make you ignore your manners.

We remember smaller landmarks too: Cedar Street Cafe at 756 Glen Huntly Road cropped up on the dog‑friendly lists, Syber’s Books at 666 Glen Huntly Rd used to be the place for second‑hand oddities (now closed, as Time Out noted), and somewhere along Glen Huntly Road the theatrical Shelly at 809 planted its flag and a lot of opinionated reviews. Marmaris II was name‑checked among local takeaways, and Mr Brightside got a nod for proper suburban breakfasts. Concrete Playground and the guides loved pointing these out when they rounded up neighbourhood gems.

Petty local drama? Our main soap opera was the scroll queue: tiny fights over the last cream‑cheese topping, seasonal specials disappearing in a heartbeat, and who got the last slice of brisket at Big Boy. Not exactly council scandals, but we’re proud of our low‑stakes theatrics.

Who would hate it here: anyone allergic to cinnamon, dogs, or small queues that turn into social events. Also people who detest hearty barbecue and the idea of a restaurant putting on a show.

Who would love it: families, dog walkers, scroll devotees and anyone who appreciates that suburban weirdness where a tiny North Road bakery once made half the city queasy with envy. We’d miss it if it were gone — and frankly, so would the rest of Melbourne.
