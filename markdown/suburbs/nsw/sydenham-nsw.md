# Sydenham (NSW)

Slug: `sydenham-nsw`

We know the bit: a tiny 20‑seat room on Unwins Bridge Road where you pretended you were on a European getaway but really you were two minutes from Sydenham Station. We used to squeeze into Sippenham for its osso buco lasagne, house‑made pasta and whatever Lo‑Fi Wines had decided was acceptable that week — whole place felt like someone’s excellent dinner party powered by a community‑sourced vinyl collection. Broadsheet, Concrete Playground — they liked to say it “put Sydenham on the map.” Fair.

Our weekend circuit used to be gloriously small‑scale. Morning: stare at the empty corner where the pasta bar’s queue snaked. Afternoon: stumble past 20 Swain Street to the General Gordon Hotel — remember when it came back from the 2018 fire and reopened in March 2021 with a new roof, a dog‑friendly courtyard, a bottle shop and a bistro with a woodfire oven? Urban List and Concrete Playground loved that comeback story almost as much as we did.

Local drama? The press preferred infrastructure gossip to us. Sydenham’s claim to fame, apparently, is transport: the station got major upgrades (two new platforms, a shiny overpass), and the City‑and‑Southwest Metro testing kept making headlines — first full test runs, door‑on‑mid‑ride footage, Gen Z obsessing over the metro like it’s a new sneaker drop. There was even talk of a 13.8‑kilometre MetroWay linking Sydenham to Bankstown for walkers and riders. Exciting if you like concrete and timetables; less exciting if you liked the old, quieter route.

Who would hate living here? If you can’t abide cosy 20‑seat pasta bars, railway upgrades, and a suburb that announces itself by being a transport node, this place will feel like someone’s hypothesis about urban living rather than a neighbourhood. If queues for lasagne and the odd test‑train noise make you cross, move along.

Who would love it? People who like a proper small‑scale wining and pasting scene (Sippenham-era devotees), pub lovers who cheered the General Gordon’s return, vinyl nerds who miss the community record pile, and anyone secretly thrilled by transit progress — the kind of people who read Concrete Playground, Broadsheet and Time Out and say, “Yeah, that’s ours.”
