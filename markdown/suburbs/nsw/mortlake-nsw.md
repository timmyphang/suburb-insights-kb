# Mortlake (NSW)

Slug: `mortlake-nsw`

Back in the day, a Saturday in Mortlake looked suspiciously like a low-key indie film: we’d roll out in the morning, half hopeful and half caffeinated, and head for Wolfpack Coffee Roasters on 12 Bertram Street — the little roastery that used to brag about onsite roasting, a minimalist Den and roast beef toasties to soak up the weekend. They even sold bags of beans for the people who liked to pretend they were home baristas.

If we weren’t pretending at home, we’d pretend at the Palace Hotel, which has always been our stalwart pub — the place that survived gentrification, uni kids, families and old folk, and still pulled crowds for generous pub food and cold beers. Or, if we were feeling culinary and slightly theatrical, there was that empty warehouse someone turned into a home for Sullo: a vintage bus, a designed car-park ordering area and the whole food-truck vibe parked where you’d least expect real food to be.

We’re proud of our practical charms. El Mercado used to sit down the end of Magnolia Drive as part-café, part-deli — Kristian Juanas and Eduardo Giraldo put it together in a way that felt like a wonky, beloved market find rather than a polished mall instalment. For families we knew, St Pat’s playgroup at 33 Gale St was a Friday morning ritual (9:15 AM, soft mats, home corner) and CityCare Inner West held sessions at Hillsong Church Inner West on Tennyson Road. If you read Mortlake Mummy back in 2013, you’ll remember the local parenting braintrust sparring over cost-of-housing questions like it was a neighbourhood sport.

Who would hate living here? People who want their suburb to loudly announce itself as “cool.” Mortlake has always been described — correctly — as an unassuming inner-west corner. If you need neon and brunch menus that read like manifestos, you’ll be uncomfortable.

Who would love it? Families who like predictable playgroup schedules, coffee obsessives who appreciated a proper roastery Den, pub regulars who value a good roast, and anyone who gets a thrill from finding a vintage bus serving dinner in a car park. We’ll take our quiet little eccentricities, thanks — and we’ll keep pretending we planned it that way.
