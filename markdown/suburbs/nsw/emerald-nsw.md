# Emerald (NSW)

Slug: `emerald-nsw`

We know the morning here: mist hanging over the lake, the ghost of a steam whistle, and us pretending the Puffing Billy actually arrives on time. Back in the day the guidebooks made a sport of it — Time Out reminding everyone that the Puffing Billy is the heritage steam railway opened in 1900 and that Emerald Lake Park was our postcard: two lakes, paddleboats, picnic lawns and very committed people with very small children trying to launch a water bicycle.

As locals we’ve been shuttled into travel pieces more than once. Broadsheet used to point visitors toward The General Food Store — yes, the one Paul ‘Dougie’ Douglas and Belinda Smullen set up — as evidence that Emerald could be both “bustling” and quietly smug about it. Concrete Playground even had us on the Open Gardens Victoria roster for that dramatic two‑acre cactus garden up at Telopea, which is the kind of place you mention at a dinner party to sound worldly and drought‑resilient.

There’s no juicy council scandal in the clippings — which is either a relief or worrying, depending on how much drama you need to feel alive. Instead the press gave us puff pieces and outing recommendations: park guides, playgroup listings (yes, the Hills Hub — we meet in a large room there), and lists that treat us like an accessory to a daytrip. If that’s our scandal, we’ll wear it.

Who would hate living here? People who need neon signage and 24/7 espresso theatrics. People who measure weekends in rooftop bars and late-night bookings would find our rhythm — paddleboats at noon, open gardens in the afternoon, a steam train lighting up the trees in winter — painfully quaint.

Who would love it? Families who actually like playgroups and picnics, anyone who thinks a miniature steam railway counts as a local cultural institution, and that rare breed who treasures small‑town weirdness: cactus gardens, seasonal light trains and a cafe with provenance that got mentioned in a Broadsheet road trip. We’re not flashy. We’re the sort of place that shows up in travel pieces to remind city folk that green exists outside their 24/7 feed — and honestly, we’re quite pleased about it.
