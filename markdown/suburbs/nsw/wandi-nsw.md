# Wandi (NSW)

Slug: `wandi-nsw`

We live in Wandi — yes, that tiny patch of postcode 6167 everyone imagines as one big backyard, but actually clocks in at about 12 km² and a population of 809. We know where every flying fox start and every kangaroo likes to sunbake, and we’ve got receipts: the community directory, thank you very much.

Morning here is a slow roll through Honeywood Estate to the Wandi Playing Fields on Litoria Drive, where the kids treat the double flying fox (complete with accessible seat and harness) like it’s a rite of passage. If you haven’t seen a toddler launch themselves off a racing slide while a local roo inspects the swings, have you really visited? (Perth Mums Group wrote it up — not our word alone.)

Weekends are either community-service chic or wood-shavings chic. We’ll pop down to Lake Magenup on Frayne Place for a community planting morning — last one promised kangaroo sightings and a lot of earnest hugging of saplings — or wander to the Wandi Community Hall at 302 De Haer Road, which the Wandi Progress Association somehow keeps humming along as our unofficial civic HQ. Need craft cred? The Wandi Woodturners run monthly competitions and trial sessions, and Textile Lab even lists “in our studio in Wandi” as a working option, so yes, we make useful things out of timber and fabric and then pretend we meant to.

There’s very little petty drama in the files — our local life is more “neighbourly meetings and Hawkers at Honeywood” than headline-grabbing scandal. The Hawkers gig (Litoria Drive, Friday 21st February, 5pm — Perth Mums Group again) is proof we like our free-entry community festivals served with a side of polite queuing.

Who would hate Wandi? If the idea of a community that actually plants things, runs a woodturning club, and counts kangaroos as part of the suburbscape fills you with existential dread, then yes, you’ll be miserable here. Also, if you can’t cope without forty cafes in a 5km radius, maybe keep scrolling.

Who would love it? Families who prize an accessible, brilliant playground; creatives who want studio space and quiet; people who prefer community meetings to anonymous apartment living. We’re small, slightly rural, improbably organised — and very proud of it.
