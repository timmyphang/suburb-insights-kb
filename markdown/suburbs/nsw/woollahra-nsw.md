# Woollahra (NSW)

Slug: `woollahra-nsw`

We like to tell visitors Woollahra is a village. The truth is more fun: it’s a well-mannered stage set where the props include 1930s Art Deco, rows of significant trees and a boutique on every corner that once made us feel very cultured.

Back in the day we’d amble down Queen Street — you know, the actual village strip Broadsheet and Time Out used to fuss over — past the Sunday Woollahra Artisan Markets and the auction-room glamour of Bonhams. If you were feeling flush you’d book a long lunch at Chiswick with its kitchen garden (Matt Moran and Peter Sullivan’s communal dining thing, as Urban List and Concrete Playground noted). If you wanted to pretend you were a local you’d nod appreciatively at the Woollahra Hotel’s art‑deco facade and its courtyard vertical garden that reviewers loved.

We’ve always had culture and civility in equal measure. Woollahra Gallery at Redleaf hosted the Small Sculpture Prize (entries opened for 2025, remember?), and Woollahra Council ran kids’ things like the ANZAC Day 2026 writing competition — yes, there were deadlines and winners announced in April 2026. Libraries kept the civic vibe alive with service points in Double Bay, Paddington and Watsons Bay. Even the trees are officially important: Woollahra’s Significant Tree Register is basically the council’s botanical VIP list.

Petty drama? Oh, we’ve got a tasteful amount. Woollahra Council once copped flak for banning dogs at Kutti Beach — a very eastern‑suburbs scandal — and there was that petition pushing back after the council forced earlier closing times on Indian Home Diner. Celebrity cameo: Chris Hemsworth was spotted at Chargrill Charlie’s, just to remind everyone we also do casual fame.

Who would hate it here? If you loathe heritage terraces, curated menus and people who treat a sculpture prize like sporting finals, this isn’t your postcode. If you want the train station yesterday, you’ll be muttering about the half‑built Woollahra Station from the 1970s — plans to finish it were floated, but patience is a local virtue.

Who would love it? Families who like markets and libraries, gardeners who appreciate listed trees, diners who relish a Chiswick long lunch and anyone who thinks small‑scale art prizes and a tidy village strip are the height of civilisation. We call it quaint, they call it community — same thing, really.
